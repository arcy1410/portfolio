Curio: a learning feed people actually use

I took Curio from idea to a live product with 120 real users in about three weeks. It's a swipeable feed of two-minute knowledge cards, each fact-checked by AI before it ships: the Reels habit, with a payoff you remember.

The short version

  • Problem

    Short video eats hours and leaves nothing. Learning content is “either baby-food or a lecture,” and AI-written content fails on trust.

  • What I built

    A swipeable feed where a second AI model checks every card against its source before anyone sees it. I ran the interviews, wrote the spec and metrics, and built it with Claude Code.

  • Result

    Activation and sign-in beat their targets. Bringing users back did not, and that became the next thing to fix.

Problem

Hours of scrolling, nothing kept

Young Indians spend two or more hours a day on short videos and come away with nothing. I interviewed three people, and all three described the same gap without being prompted.

  • The gapContent is “either baby-food or a lecture,” with nothing in between for an intelligent beginner.
  • The trust gateFor AI-written content, trust is pass or fail.
  • The habit“My thumb just did it.” Nobody needs to be taught to swipe.
  • The real driverWanting to be “interesting outside my one thing,” not guilt after scrolling.
I don't have an attention problem, I have a trust problem. One wrong claim and I'm out.Interviewee, discovery round

The interviews changed my thesis

I started out betting on guilt after scrolling, but one interviewee rejected it outright. The stronger driver was wanting to be “interesting outside my one thing.” So Curio keeps the swipe and changes what it pays: short, sourced cards that treat people as intelligent beginners.

The sample is small: three people, all men aged 24 to 28. I named that as a limitation, and the next round targets the younger half of the 18 to 30 audience.

What I built

A feed you can trust, by design

Curio has been live since 22 July 2026, as a web app that also installs on phones. Every card is checked claim by claim against its source before anyone can read it.

The trust pipeline

  1. A real sourceEach card is drawn from a real source, such as a Wikipedia article.
  2. Writer modelOne AI model writes a two-minute card from it.
  3. Checker modelA second model checks the card claim by claim against the source.
  4. Database gateA card that fails never reaches users: the database itself makes unverified cards unreadable.

64 verified cards across six topics were live by Demo Day. Every card opens as a question, so reading becomes recall practice rather than passive scrolling.

What else ships

  • Personalized feedTopic weights shift with each swipe, and the feed visibly tunes toward your interests after about 10 swipes.
  • Typed quizzesGuess the answer, get judged instantly, then see the sourced explanation.
  • Kept pile with spaced repetitionSaved cards fade and resurface for review, instead of piling up unread.
  • Self-refilling feedWhen you run out, Curio writes and fact-checks 10 new cards for your interests on demand.
  • Anonymous-first sign-inGoogle sign-in only after 3 swipes, plus a mocked ₹149-a-month premium tier.
  • Analytics from day onePostHog with 37 named events, so every metric below comes from production data.

Built with React, Supabase, Vercel and PostHog, using Claude Code.

Key decisions

What I chose not to optimize for

The hardest calls were about what not to optimize. The North Star rewards facts that stick, not swipes.

DecisionWhy
North Star: cards retained per weekly active user, not swipes or time spentCurio promises facts that stick. A metric that rewards 200 forgotten swipes would turn it into the thing it replaces
Session length is a ceiling (6-minute median), not a targetA learning feed should let you leave on time. Longer sessions count as failure
No streak-loss guilt or fear-of-missing-out notificationsThe product reuses the Reels habit loop, so I drew a clear line against dark patterns
The fact-checker must be a reasoning modelFaster models flagged true claims as unsupported on long sources, such as a phrase quoted word for word from Wikipedia. A reasoning model got it right, at about 7 seconds instead of 1
Unverified cards blocked by the database, not by the appTrust is pass or fail, so it had to be a rule nothing can bypass
Sign-in moved from 7 swipes to 3, kept only if 30% or more convertEarlier sign-in saves more users' data; below 30% it costs more users than it keeps
Fix personalization with more content, not a new algorithmAnalysis showed 74% of simulated users ran out of their favourite topic within 9 swipes. Ten cards per topic took that to 0%

What the data taught me

Three things the numbers showed

The biggest leak comes before the first tap, and running out of cards, not boredom, ended sessions.

  1. The biggest leak comes before the first tap. 25% of visitors left on the intro screen without touching a topic (114 down to 85). They never reached the choices, so the fix is to open on the topic picker, which is the next A/B test.
  2. The North Star reads near zero, and that is the finding. 27 people saved cards and only 4 came back to one, because nothing brings users back yet. I shipped the recall quiz and the self-refilling feed, and saves rose from 8 to 29 in the last week.
  3. Running out of cards was the real churn. Most pilot users read the whole library, so sessions ended from exhaustion, not boredom. I moved card generation to the moment of demand.

Two real testers also found two sign-in bugs that desktop testing had missed. Both were fixed the same day.

What's next

Where Curio goes from here

Fix the first screen, give people a reason to come back without guilt, and widen the interviews.

  • Test opening on the topic picker, the next A/B test.
  • Build a reason to return that doesn't rely on guilt-based notifications.
  • Run five to seven more interviews with younger users and women.
